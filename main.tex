\section{Introduction}
Magnetic field sensors are ubiquitous in modern technology, finding applications ranging from automotive systems and data storage to biomedical diagnostics. While traditional devices based on the Hall effect or anisotropic magnetoresistance (AMR) are widely used, next-generation spintronic sensors exploiting phenomena such as giant magnetoresistance (GMR) and tunneling magnetoresistance (TMR) have been developed to achieve higher sensitivities \cite{farwa_2026}. However, the complex manufacturing processes and inherent noise in TMR sensors have driven the search for simpler, robust alternatives. 

Recently, the Spin Hall Magnetoresistance (SMR) effect has emerged as a promising mechanism for magnetic sensing. SMR arises in bilayers consisting of a heavy metal (HM) with strong spin-orbit coupling and a magnetic layer, typically an insulator like yttrium iron garnet (YIG) or a conductive ferromagnet (FM). The effect originates from the simultaneous action of the Spin Hall Effect (SHE) and the Inverse Spin Hall Effect (ISHE) \cite{chen2016theory}. A charge current in the HM generates a transverse spin current, which interacts with the adjacent magnetization via spin-transfer torque. The reflected spin current is converted back into a charge current, modulating the electrical resistance based on the magnetization orientation \cite{chen2016theory, baldrati2018full, fischer2018spin}.

While SMR provides a non-invasive tool to measure magnetization direction, its integration into conductive HM/FM bilayers (e.g., Pt/FeCoB, Ta/FeCoB) introduces a significant complication: the simultaneous presence of AMR in the FM layer. The total resistance of the bilayer becomes a parallel combination of the SMR-modulated HM resistance and the AMR-modulated FM resistance \cite{farwa_2026}. Accurately separating these two contributions is critical for optimizing sensor performance, specifically in Wheatstone bridge configurations designed to minimize power consumption while maximizing sensitivity.

In this paper, we critically evaluate existing analytical models used to separate SMR and AMR using in-plane magnetoresistance measurements. We show that these models fail to decouple the physical phenomena due to inherent parallel resistance weighting factors. Subsequently, we propose a novel hysteresis model based on Stoner-Wohlfarth energy minimization over a multi-domain ensemble, successfully capturing the complex angular dependence of the resistance in both isotropic and anisotropic regimes.

\section{Literature Review: SMR and AMR in Heterostructures}
The disentanglement of Spin Hall Magnetoresistance (SMR) and Anisotropic Magnetoresistance (AMR) has been a subject of intense research since the theoretical prediction and experimental verification of SMR. This section reviews the fundamental developments in SMR theory, its extension to antiferromagnetic systems, and the current state-of-the-art in SMR-based sensing technology.

\subsection{Fundamental Mechanism and Symmetry}
The theoretical foundation of SMR was rigorously established by Nakayama \textit{et al.} \cite{nakayama2013spin} and Chen \textit{et al.} \cite{chen2016theory}, who described SMR using spin diffusion theory and quantum mechanical boundary conditions. Unlike bulk AMR, which arises from spin-orbit coupling (SOC) affecting the scattering of conduction electrons into localized $d$-states within a magnetic conductor \cite{mcguire1975anisotropic}, SMR is an interfacial phenomenon. It depends on the spin Hall angle, spin diffusion length, and spin-mixing conductance ($G_{\uparrow\downarrow}$) at the heavy metal/magnetic layer interface.

In all-metallic bilayers such as Pt/Co or W/CoFeB, both effects coexist, making separation challenging. Kim \textit{et al.} \cite{kim2016spin} demonstrated that SMR in metallic bilayers can be significantly larger than in insulators, providing a model for spin absorption in the metal. Avci \textit{et al.} \textit{et al.} \cite{avci2015magnetoresistance} performed a critical comparison between heavy metals (Pt, Ta, W) and light metals (Cu, Ti) to isolate SMR signals from standard AMR, establishing that the transverse ($yz$-plane) rotation serves as a unique "fingerprint" for SMR, as conventional AMR should remain constant in this geometry for polycrystalline films.

\subsection{SMR in Antiferromagnetic Insulators}
A significant milestone in spintronics is the observation of SMR in antiferromagnetic (AFM) insulators, which allows for the electrical detection of the Néel vector ($\mathbf{n}$) without macroscopic magnetization. Seminal work by Hoogeboom \textit{et al.} \cite{hoogeboom2017negative} and Baldrati \textit{et al.} \cite{baldrati2018full} demonstrated that NiO/Pt bilayers exhibit a "negative" SMR signal, characterized by a 90$^\circ$ phase shift compared to ferromagnetic systems. This shift occurs because the Néel vector in easy-plane AFMs like NiO aligns perpendicular to the applied magnetic field (above the spin-flop field) to minimize Zeeman energy.

Baldrati \textit{et al.} \cite{baldrati2018full, baldrati2018anisotropic} provided comprehensive 3D angular dependent studies of epitaxial NiO(001)/Pt, showing that SMR can track the redistribution of AFM domains (S- and T-domains) under high magnetic fields. Fischer \textit{et al.} \textit{et al.} \cite{fischer2018spin} further investigated these multidomain effects, noting a quadratic increase in SMR amplitude with the applied field and utilizing SMR to estimate magnetoelastic destressing fields. Additionally, studies on Pt/NiO/YIG trilayers \cite{lin2016antiferromagnetic, hou2017tunable} have revealed that thin NiO layers can enhance spin current transmission through magnetic fluctuations.

\subsection{Multi-Domain Modeling and Hysteresis}
Real thin films often deviate from the "macrospin" assumption due to their polycrystalline nature and grain boundaries. To address this, multi-domain models based on the ensemble average of Stoner-Wohlfarth particles have been developed. Althammer \textit{et al.} \cite{althammer2013quantitative} established the quantitative framework for SMR in such systems. More recently, Magni \textit{et al.} \cite{magni2022spin} and our previous work \cite{farwa_2026} incorporated domain wall motion and non-uniform magnetization using a "truncated astroid" approach to effectively reproduce the hysteresis curves of sensors. These models are essential for interpreting non-saturated signals and the complex interplay between anisotropy and Zeeman fields in device geometries.

\subsection{State-of-the-Art SMR Sensors}
Current state-of-the-art SMR sensors are approaching the performance levels of established AMR and TMR technologies. Recent prototypes have achieved detectivities in the range of 0.7 nT/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ to 1 nT/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ at 1 Hz \cite{xu2018ultrathin, xu2019spin}. A critical advancement in noise mitigation is the use of AC excitation, which successfully suppresses $1/f$ noise. Furthermore, unlike TMR sensors that require external bias magnets for linearization, modern SMR sensors leverage Spin-Orbit Torque (SOT) for built-in linearization, leading to devices with near-zero offset and minimal hysteresis. These advancements have opened doors for applications in rapid point-of-care biomedical diagnostics and high-resolution industrial non-destructive testing.

\section{Analytical Models for SMR and AMR Separation}
Consider a conductive HM/FM bilayer where the AMR and SMR effects coexist since the current that goes into bilayer splits into two parts $j_{HM}$ and $j_{FM}$. So we have two conduction channels in the bilayer and the total resistance of the bilayer is a parallel combination of two channels. The AMR in the FM layer depends on the angle between the magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ and the current density $\mathbf{j}$, while the SMR in the HM layer depends on the angle of $\mathbf{M}$ with respect to the transverse direction $\mathbf{t}$. 
The overall resistance of the HM/FM bilayer with two conduction channels in parallel is:
% The total parallel resistance exhibits a baseline and an angular amplitude modulated by the applied field $\mathbf{H}$.
\begin{equation}
    R = R_{\parallel} + \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{FM}^2}\Delta R_{AMR}m_{j}^2 - \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{HM}^2}\Delta R_{SMR}m_{t}^2
\end{equation}
Where $m_j$ is the component of magnetization in the $j$-direction, $m_t$ is the component of magnetization in the $t$-direction, $R_{\parallel}$ represents the parallel combination of $R_{HM}$ and $R_{FM}$, and $\Delta R_{AMR}$ and $\Delta R_{SMR}$ are the contributions coming from the AMR and SMR effects, respectively.

\subsection{Saturated Magnetization Model}
In the simplest approach, often referred to as the saturated model, the magnetization $M$ is assumed to have a constant magnitude $M_{s}$. Under this assumption, any observed variation in resistance is attributed entirely to the coherent rotation of $M$ relative to the current direction $j$. How magnetization reaches saturation is irrelevant which means this model is, in general, valid for both reversibility and non-reversibility cases. If $\alpha$ represents the angle between the applied current $j$ and magnetization, then $m_j = \cos \alpha$ and $m_t = \sin \alpha$. The total resistance, under approximation $\Delta R_{AMR}, \Delta R_{SMR} \ll R_{HM}, R_{FM}$, can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    R \approx R_0 + A\cos^2 \alpha
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    R_0 &= \left( R_{\parallel} - \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{HM}^2}\Delta R_{SMR} \right) \quad  \\
    A &= \left( \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{FM}^2}\Delta R_{AMR} + \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{HM}^2}\Delta R_{SMR} \right) \quad 
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
    R_{\parallel} = \frac{R_{HM}R_{FM}}{R_{HM}+R_{FM}}
\end{equation}

% \begin{equation}
%     R_{total}(\theta_M) \approx R_{baseline} + \Delta R_{total} \cos(2\theta_M)
% \end{equation}
The $R_0$ represents constant baseline resistance (independent of field H), the angular amplitude $A$ is a weighted combination of $\Delta R_{AMR}$ and $\Delta R_{SMR}$ and $R_{\parallel}$ the parallel resistance of the layers. At saturation, the magnetization perfectly tracks the applied field ($\alpha = \theta$). However, for fields below saturation, defining $\alpha$ without independent vector magnetometry is impossible.

\subsection{Reversible Magnetization Model}
An alternative model (the reversible model) assumes that $\mathbf{M}$ is always strictly parallel to $\mathbf{H}$, a property more akin to paramagnetism. The theoretical approach relies on the following idealized assumptions regarding the sample's magnetic behavior:\\
Isotropy: The sample is magnetically isotropic, possessing no intrinsic preferred direction for magnetization.\\ Collinearity: The magnetization vector strictly aligns with the applied magnetic field ($\vec{M} \parallel \vec{H}$) and is defined as $\vec{M} = M_s f(H) \hat{H}$. Here, $M_s$ represents the saturation magnetization, $\hat{H}$ represents direction of the applied field, and $f(H)$ is a single-valued function mapping the field magnitude to a fractional value between $0$ and $1$.\\ Anhysteretic Response: The system exhibits no hysteresis and possesses zero remanence. To satisfy this condition, the magnitude function $f(H)$ is perfectly symmetric ($f(-H) = f(H)$) and drops to zero in the absence of an applied field ($f(0) = 0$).\\
Under these assumptions, when the magnetic field is applied at an angle $\theta$ with respect to the current direction $j$:
\begin{equation}
    m_j = f(H)\cos\theta \quad ; \quad m_t = f(H)\sin\theta
\end{equation}

The overall resistance of the bilayer is:
\begin{equation}
    R \approx R_0 + A\cos^2 \theta
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{align}
    R_0(H) &=  R_{\parallel} - [f(H)]^2 \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{HM}^2}\Delta R_{SMR} \\
    A(H) &= [f(H)]^2 \left( \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{FM}^2}\Delta R_{AMR} + \frac{R_{\parallel}^2}{R_{HM}^2}\Delta R_{SMR} \right)
\end{align}

It is found, at saturation, the reversible model theoretically achieves geometric separation of the AMR and SMR functions under orthognal field alignments ($\theta=0^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$), it fundamentally fails to yield physical separation in practical ferromagnetic bilayers. This discrepancy arises from three critical limitations: (1) it neglects ferromagnetic anisotropy, assuming magnetization strictly tracks the applied field; (2) the magnetization fraction $f(H)$ is unknown without independent magnetometry; and crucially, (3) the parallel resistance weighting factors ($R_{FM}, R_{HM}$) cannot be independently measured post-fabrication. Consequently, extracting isolated $\Delta R_{AMR}$ and $\Delta R_{SMR}$ values necessitates supplementary diagnostic techniques beyond this geometric model.

\subsection{Comparison}
A key finding of our theoretical analysis emerges from a direct comparison between the saturated model and the reversible model. By evaluating the angular amplitude $A(H)$ in both frameworks, we establish that the reversible model scales the saturated amplitude by the square of the magnetization fraction, $f(H)$:$$A^{\text{Rev}}(H) = [f(H)]^2 A^{\text{Sat}}(H)$$As the system approaches magnetic saturation ($\theta = \alpha$), the fractional function approaches unity ($f(H) \to 1$). Consequently, the amplitude of the reversible model converges perfectly with that of the saturated model ($A^{\text{Rev}}(H) \to A^{\text{Sat}}(H)$). This mathematical relationship confirms that the saturated model represents the strict saturated limit of the more generalized reversible model.

\section{Proposed Multi-Domain Hysteresis Model}
To capture the true physical behavior, we propose a multi-domain hysteresis model for both isotropic and anisotropic samples. We discretize the FM layer into $N$ non-interacting single-domain grains. Each grain possesses a local saturation magnetization $M_s$ and a unique uniaxial easy-axis direction. The local magnetization angle $\alpha_{i}$ (with respect to j direction) of the $i$-th grain is determined by minimizing the Stoner-Wohlfarth energy, defined by the competition between the local anisotropy and the applied Zeeman field.

\subsection{Resistance Formulation}
Assuming current flows through the sample such that the macroscopic domains are effectively in series, the resistance of the $i$-th domain comprises a parallel combination of the local FM section (exhibiting AMR) and the underlying HM section (exhibiting SMR). For a single domain (grain-$i$) with a local magnetization angle $\alpha_i$, the anisotropic magnetoresistance (AMR) contribution within the ferromagnetic (FM) layer is given by:
\begin{equation}
    R_{FM,i}(\alpha_i) = R_{FM} + \Delta R_{AMR}\cos^2\alpha_i
\end{equation}

Similarly, the spin Hall magnetoresistance (SMR) contribution from the heavy metal (HM) section directly underneath grain-$i$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    R_{HM,i}(\alpha_i) = R_{HM} - \Delta R_{SMR}\sin^2\alpha_i
\end{equation}

In this framework, the baseline resistances ($R_{FM}$, $R_{HM}$) and the maximum resistance changes ($\Delta R_{AMR}$, $\Delta R_{SMR}$) are constant across all domains (same material), whereas the angular dependencies ($\cos^2\alpha_i$ and $\sin^2\alpha_i$) vary based on the local magnetization direction with respect to local easy axis.

To determine the combined resistance of grain-$i$, we treat the FM and HM sections as parallel resistors. Under the standard approximation that the magnitude of the magnetoresistance is much smaller than the baseline resistances ($\Delta R \ll R_{FM}, R_{HM}$), the combined local resistance $R_i$ simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
    R_i = R_{\parallel} + \psi_{AMR}\cos^2(\alpha_i) - \psi_{SMR}\sin^2(\alpha_i)
\end{equation}

where the effective AMR and SMR amplitudes ($\psi_{AMR}$ and $\psi_{SMR}$) and the baseline parallel resistance ($R_{\parallel}$) are defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{AMR} = \frac{R_{\parallel}^2 \Delta R_{AMR}}{R_{FM}^2} \quad ; \quad \psi_{SMR} = \frac{R_{\parallel}^2 \Delta R_{SMR}}{R_{HM}^2} \quad ; \quad R_{\parallel} = \frac{R_{FM}R_{HM}}{R_{FM}+R_{HM}}
\end{equation}
% Assuming current flows such that the macroscopic domains are effectively in series, the resistance of the $i$-th domain comprises a parallel combination of the local FM section (exhibiting AMR) and the underlying HM section (exhibiting SMR):
% \begin{equation}
%     R_i = \frac{R_{FM,i}(\theta_{M,i}) R_{HM,i}(\theta_{M,i})}{R_{FM,i}(\theta_{M,i}) + R_{HM,i}(\theta_{M,i})}
% \end{equation}
% The AMR contribution is $R_{FM,i} = R_{FM}^0 + \Delta R_{AMR} \cos^2(\theta_{M,i})$, and the SMR contribution is $R_{HM,i} = R_{HM}^0 - \Delta R_{SMR} \sin^2(\theta_{M,i})$. 

The normalized resistance of grain i assuming all grains are of same size, so each grain contribute 1/N of total contribution is:
\begin{equation}
    R_i = \frac{1}{N} \left[ R_{\parallel}^{total} + \psi_{AMR}^{total}\cos^2(\alpha_i) - \psi_{SMR}^{total}\sin^2(\alpha_i) \right]
\end{equation}

By summing this normalized expression over all $N$ grains, the unphysical $N$-dependence cancels out. The total resistance of the sample is then naturally expressed in terms of the ensemble averages of the angular distributions ($\langle \cos^2 \alpha \rangle$ and $\langle \sin^2 \alpha \rangle$):
\begin{equation}
    R = \sum_{i=1}^N R_i(\alpha_i) = R_{\parallel}^{total} + \psi_{AMR}^{total} \langle \cos^2 \alpha \rangle - \psi_{SMR}^{total} \langle \sin^2 \alpha \rangle
\end{equation}
Here, $\psi_{AMR}$ and $\psi_{SMR}$ denote the effective (weighted) magnetoresistance amplitudes, while $\langle \cos^2\alpha \rangle$ and $\langle \sin^2\alpha \rangle$ represent the statistical averages of the angular dependencies calculated over the entire domain distribution.\\
% \begin{equation}
%     R_{total}(H) = R_{||} + A \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \cos(2\theta_{M,i}(H))
% \end{equation}
In the continuum limit, the ensemble averages for a multi-domain sample are replaced by an integral weighted by the easy-axis distribution function $P(\phi)$:
\begin{align}
    \langle \cos^2\alpha \rangle &= \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2\alpha_{eq} \, P(\varphi)d\varphi \\
    \langle \sin^2\alpha \rangle &= \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2\alpha_{eq} \, P(\varphi)d\varphi
\end{align}

where $P(\varphi)$ is the probability distribution of the easy axis angles for a multi-domain sample, and $\alpha_{eq}$ is the equilibrium magnetization angle of a grain whose easy axis is oriented at an angle $\varphi$, with the external field applied at an angle $\theta$. The integrals are evaluated over the range from $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ to $+\frac{\pi}{2}$ because this span completely captures all unique easy axis angles for a uniaxial system. 

The equilibrium angle $\alpha_{eq}$ is calculated by minimizing the energy equation:
\begin{equation}
    E(\alpha) = K\sin^2(\alpha - \varphi) - \mu_0 M_s\cos(\alpha - \theta)
\end{equation}

Substituting the continuous averages, the complete model equation is:
\begin{equation}
    { R = R_{\parallel} + \psi_{AMR} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2\alpha_{eq} \, P(\varphi)d\varphi - \psi_{SMR} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2\alpha_{eq} \, P(\varphi)d\varphi }
\end{equation}
% \begin{equation}
%     \langle \cos(2\theta_M) \rangle = \int f(\phi) \cos(2\theta_M(\phi, H)) d\phi
% \end{equation}

\subsection{Isotropic Sample}
To model an ideally isotropic sample, we assume the macroscopic magnetic behavior exhibits no preferred orientation. At the microscopic level, this implies that the individual single-domain grains are completely randomly oriented. Consequently, the local easy axis angles ($\varphi$), defined with respect to the charge current direction $j$, follow a uniform (flat) probability distribution over the physically unique range of $[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$. To ensure the probability is conserved over this interval, the normalized distribution function is a constant:\begin{equation}P(\varphi) = \frac{1}{\pi} \quad \text{for} \quad \varphi \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\end{equation}By substituting this uniform distribution into our general continuous model, the integral over the probability function simplifies, and the total resistance for the isotropic sample becomes:\begin{equation}R_{iso} = R_{\parallel} + \frac{\psi_{AMR}}{\pi} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2\alpha_{eq} , d\varphi - \frac{\psi_{SMR}}{\pi} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2\alpha_{eq} , d\varphi\end{equation}
At the physical extremes of zero field and complete saturation, the isotropic model can be easily evaluated. However, extracting the continuous resistance curve across intermediate fields is non-trivial, requiring Stoner-Wohlfarth energy minimization to numerically compute the requisite statistical averages.
% \textbf{Isotropic Limit:} If the sample has no preferred macroscopic anisotropy, $f(\phi)$ is a uniform flat distribution over $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$. At zero field ($H=0$), the easy axes are randomly distributed, yielding $\langle \cos(2\theta_M) \rangle = 0$. Thus, $R(0) = R_{||}$, meaning both SMR and AMR contribute equally, placing the zero-field resistance exactly at the midpoint of the full resistance envelope.

\subsection{Anisotropic Sample}
Conversely, to model an anisotropic sample where the individual grains exhibit a macroscopic preferred direction (e.g., from field-annealing or shape anisotropy), the easy axis angles are no longer uniform. Instead, it is assumed that the distribution of easy axes follows a Gaussian probability distribution centered around a macroscopic preferred direction, $\varphi_0$:\begin{equation}P(\varphi) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[ - \frac{(\varphi - \varphi_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right]\end{equation}Here, the parameter $\sigma$ defines the width of the distribution and acts as an inverse measure of the macroscopic anisotropy strength; a smaller $\sigma$ indicates a highly textured sample with tightly aligned easy axes.By substituting this specific probability density function into our established continuous model framework, the ensemble averages are now weighted by this Gaussian distribution. The complete, explicit model equation for the total resistance of the anisotropic sample becomes:\begin{multline}R_{aniso} = R_{\parallel} + \frac{\psi_{AMR}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2\alpha_{eq} \exp\left[ - \frac{(\varphi - \varphi_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right] d\varphi - \- \frac{\psi_{SMR}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{+\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2\alpha_{eq} \exp\left[ - \frac{(\varphi - \varphi_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right] d\varphi\end{multline}
In practice, generating the macroscopic resistance curve $R(H, \theta)$ for any given applied field strength and direction requires a sequential numerical procedure. First, for every local easy axis angle $\varphi$, the equilibrium magnetization angle $\alpha_{eq}(H, \theta, \varphi)$ is determined by minimizing the local Stoner-Wohlfarth energy $E(\alpha)$. Second, the corresponding angular dependencies, $\cos^2\alpha_{eq}$ and $\sin^2\alpha_{eq}$, are evaluated for each grain. Third, these local contributions are integrated over the entire sample space, weighted by the anisotropic probability distribution $P(\varphi)$, to compute the macroscopic ensemble averages $\langle \cos^2\alpha_{eq} \rangle$ and $\langle \sin^2\alpha_{eq} \rangle$. Finally, these integrated averages are substituted into the overarching transport equation to compute the total field- and angle-dependent resistance of the bilayer.\\
% \textbf{Anisotropic Limit:} If the sample possesses a macroscopic preferred direction (e.g., from field-annealing or shape anisotropy), $f(\phi)$ follows a Gaussian distribution centered around the preferred axis. If the preferred axis is parallel to the current $\mathbf{j}$, the zero-field resistance shifts upward towards the high-resistance AMR state. Conversely, if the easy axis is transverse to the current, the zero-field resistance shifts downward towards the high-resistance SMR state.

\section{Discussion}
The proposed hysteresis model for an isotropic sample demonstrates that the resistance model equation yields straightforward analytical solutions at zero field ($H=0$). The perfectly random distribution of domains leads to exact ensemble averages of $\langle \cos^2\alpha \rangle = \langle \sin^2\alpha \rangle = 1/2$. This yields a simple zero-field baseline resistance of $R(H=0) = R_{\parallel} + \frac{\psi_{AMR}}{2} - \frac{\psi_{SMR}}{2}$. Similarly, at complete magnetic saturation ($H \to \infty$), all local magnetic moments are forced into coherent alignment with the applied field, allowing direct calculation of the saturated resistance states ($R_{H \parallel j}^{Sat}=R_{up}= R_{\parallel} + \psi_{AMR}$ and $R_{H \parallel t}^{Sat} = R_{down}=R_{\parallel} - \psi_{SMR}$). However, predicting the resistance at intermediate fields is highly non-trivial. Because the domains rotate incoherently, there is no simple analytical expression. Instead, generating the continuous field-dependent resistance curve requires a numerical approach.\\
A key diagnostic feature of the isotropic multi-domain model is the relative position of its zero-field resistance. Mathematically, the model dictates that for a perfectly random distribution of domains, the zero-field resistance ($R_{H=0}$) sits exactly halfway between the upper ($R_{up}$) and lower ($R_{down}$) saturated resistance states. This relationship is expressed simply in terms of the total measurable resistance amplitude ($\Delta R$ or $A$):
\begin{equation}
R_{H=0} = R_{down} + \frac{1}{2}\Delta R
\end{equation}
Any experimental deviation from this exact midpoint serves as a direct, observable indicator of macroscopic magnetic anisotropy within the sample.\\
However, the critical divergence between the two models emerges in the zero-field state ($H=0$). In a textured, anisotropic sample, the local easy axes are not randomly distributed but rather cluster around a macroscopic preferred direction ($\varphi_0$). As a result, the zero-field ensemble averages deviate from the ideal value of $1/2$, fundamentally shifting the baseline resistance away from the exact midpoint of $\Delta R$. This shift is dictated by the orientation of the macroscopic easy axis:Longitudinal Anisotropy ($\varphi_0 \parallel j$): If the preferred direction aligns with the charge current, a majority of the domains relax to states near $j$. This geometric clustering dictates that $\langle \cos^2\alpha \rangle_{H=0} > 1/2$ and $\langle \sin^2\alpha \rangle_{H=0} < 1/2$. Physically, the AMR contribution disproportionately dominates the zero-field state, pushing $R_{H=0}$ above the midpoint and closer to $R_{up}$.Transverse Anisotropy ($\varphi_0 \perp j$): Conversely, if the preferred direction is transverse to the current (often denoted as $\varphi_0 = t$), domains preferentially align orthogonally to $j$. In this scenario, $\langle \cos^2\alpha \rangle_{H=0} < 1/2$ and $\langle \sin^2\alpha \rangle_{H=0} > 1/2$. The SMR contribution now dominates, pulling $R_{H=0}$ below the midpoint and closer to $R_{down}$.This asymmetrical shift in the zero-field resistance serves as a powerful, observable diagnostic tool for determining the strength and direction of a sample's macroscopic magnetic texture. However, it also highlights a fundamental analytical limitation. Because the zero-field resistance is a complex convolution of the intrinsic magnetoresistive amplitudes ($\psi_{AMR}$, $\psi_{SMR}$) and the unknown, distribution-dependent angular averages, the exact quantification of isolated AMR and SMR contributions is physically impossible using only the $H=0$ state. Disentangling these variables strictly requires measurements extended into the fully saturated regime.
The proposed hysteresis model demonstrates that the zero-field resistance and the shape of the magnetoresistance loops are intimately tied to the distribution of magnetic easy axes. Unlike the reversible analytical model, our approach intrinsically incorporates magnetic remanence and hysteresis. The Stoner-Wohlfarth energy minimization for each grain correctly predicts the deviation of $\mathbf{M}$ from $\mathbf{H}$, thereby explaining the failure of paramagnetic assumptions in previous separation attempts. 

Furthermore, our model shows that at full saturation ($H \to \infty$), all domains align with the applied field regardless of their local easy axes, converging to the saturated analytical model. However, at intermediate fields, the parallel resistance weighting and the domain distribution completely dictate the measured signal, confirming that without independent transport measurements of the isolated constituent layers, pure physical separation of SMR and AMR is fundamentally constrained.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented a comprehensive theoretical framework analyzing the separation of Spin Hall Magnetoresistance and Anisotropic Magnetoresistance in conductive HM/FM bilayers. We demonstrated that standard analytical models fail to physically decouple these effects due to the inseparable parallel conduction channels and the non-collinearity of magnetization and applied field in ferromagnets. By implementing a multi-domain Stoner-Wohlfarth hysteresis model, we successfully captured the dependence of the magnetoresistance on the easy-axis distribution. This model provides an essential tool for interpreting complex in-plane magnetoresistance measurements and designing highly sensitive, optimized SMR-based sensors.
